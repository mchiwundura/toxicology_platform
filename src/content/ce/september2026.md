# Speaker Background

Michelle Mandeya is an MBChB candidate at the University of Zimbabwe.

## Session Summary

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is common across Africa, often silent until late, and costly for patients and families. This session covered how common CKD is, what drives it in Zimbabwe, why it keeps progressing, and five practical levers for improving life for people living with it.

### What CKD Is

CKD is abnormal kidney structure or function lasting more than 3 months. It is defined by a GFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or markers of damage such as albuminuria, and is staged G1 to G5, where G5 (GFR below 15) is kidney failure.

The kidney has seven jobs, and losing each one produces a recognisable problem:

| Kidney function | Consequence when lost |
|---|---|
| Waste removal | Uraemia |
| Salt and water balance | Oedema, hypertension |
| Acid removal | Metabolic acidosis |
| Potassium excretion | Hyperkalaemia |
| Phosphate excretion | Hyperphosphataemia |
| Vitamin D activation | Low calcium, bone disease |
| Erythropoietin (EPO) production | Anaemia |

### How Common Is CKD?

- **13.7%** of adults across community studies in Africa have CKD stages 1 to 5 (2026 meta-analysis), and **5.1%** have stages 3 to 5, the advanced range with real clinical consequences.
- A 2018 meta-analysis of 22 general-population studies found **15.8%**, and a 2014 review found 13.9%.
- A 2019 study of over 8,000 people across four African countries found **10.7%** among adults aged 40 to 60.

Zimbabwe-specific population data are limited, so regional figures are the best available proxy. They fit what is seen on the wards: CKD alongside diabetes, hypertension, HIV and amyloidosis.

### What Drives CKD Here

- **Hypertension:** hardens the small afferent arterioles, so glomeruli become ischaemic and scar. Kidney damage then raises blood pressure further.
- **Diabetes:** high glucose raises pressure inside the glomerulus, so the filter leaks albumin and scars.
- **HIV:** infects podocytes and causes collapsing FSGS (HIVAN). APOL1 variants raise risk, and tenofovir can injure tubules.
- **Infection and obstruction:** TB, schistosomiasis, stones, BPH and cervical cancer scar or block the urinary tract.
- **Glomerular and systemic disease:** glomerulonephritis, lupus, myeloma and amyloidosis (AA type follows chronic infection or inflammation).
- **Nephrotoxic drugs:** long-term NSAIDs and other nephrotoxins reduce renal blood flow and injure the interstitium.

### Why It Keeps Getting Worse

Once nephrons are lost from any cause, the surviving nephrons overwork. The afferent arteriole dilates, increasing flow and pressure per glomerulus (hyperfiltration). Podocytes stretch and detach, protein leaks, and glomeruli scar. GFR falls and the remaining nephrons work harder still. This cycle repeats even after the original cause has been removed.

Leaked protein is itself toxic to the tubules, so proteinuria is both a marker and a driver of progression. This is why blood pressure and proteinuria are treated early, not just the original disease.

### What CKD Does to a Life

- **Physical:** fatigue and breathlessness (anaemia, fluid overload), itching, nausea, poor appetite, swelling.
- **Treatment burden:** long trips to dialysis centres, hours on a machine several times a week, strict diet and fluid limits.
- **Financial:** dialysis and specialist care are costly and not always accessible outside major cities, leaving families facing catastrophic costs and lost income.
- **Psychosocial:** anxiety, depression, loss of independence and work, and strain on relationships and family roles.

### Solutions: Five Levers

**1. Find it early**

Early CKD is silent, and by the time a patient feels unwell much of the kidney function may already be gone.

- **Who:** people with hypertension, diabetes or HIV, a family history of kidney disease, chronic infection such as TB, or long-term NSAID use.
- **How:** urine dipstick or albumin-creatinine ratio, plus serum creatinine to estimate GFR. Both are cheap and already available.
- **Where:** add it to the HIV, hypertension and diabetes clinics patients already attend. This is one extra test, not a new programme.
- **By whom:** nurses and primary care staff trained to screen, interpret and refer.

**2. Slow the decline**

- *Control the drivers:* aim for blood pressure below about 130/80; use an ACE inhibitor or ARB when there is proteinuria; achieve glycaemic control in diabetes; give antiretroviral therapy for HIV and treat TB and other infections; relieve obstruction (stones, BPH, strictures).
- *Protect the kidney:* avoid NSAIDs and other nephrotoxins and adjust drug doses to eGFR; reduce salt, stay active and avoid smoking; check potassium and creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB; consider SGLT2 inhibitors, which slow progression in many patients where affordable; monitor GFR and urine protein regularly.

**3. Treat the complications**

| Complication | Approach |
|---|---|
| Anaemia | Check and replace iron; erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (EPO) where available; look for other causes such as bleeding or B12 or folate deficiency |
| Fluid and blood pressure | Salt restriction and diuretics; dialysis if overload does not respond |
| Acidosis | Oral sodium bicarbonate to correct low bicarbonate |
| High potassium | Limit dietary potassium, review drugs that raise it, treat severe rises as an emergency |
| Bone and mineral disease | Control phosphate (diet and binders); manage calcium and vitamin D |

**4. Access to kidney care**

- *Dialysis closer to home:* peritoneal dialysis needs less infrastructure and can be done at home after training; satellite haemodialysis units beyond the main cities; transport and cost support for patients on treatment.
- *Transplantation:* best long-term survival and quality of life for suitable patients; needs donor pathways and a steady supply of immunosuppressants; early referral and work-up.
- *Conservative kidney care:* for patients who cannot access or do not want dialysis; symptom control for itch, nausea, breathlessness and pain; palliative care and advance care planning. It is a legitimate choice, not "no treatment".

**5. Support the whole person**

- **Education and self-management:** teach diet, fluid limits, medicines and warning signs, led by nurses and dietitians, in English, Shona and Ndebele, with simple picture-based materials.
- **Mental health:** screen routinely for depression and anxiety, offer counselling and link to community mental-health services.
- **Peer and community support:** patient support groups, community health workers, and church and community networks that help with transport, meals and encouragement.
- **Financial and social protection:** subsidised medicines and dialysis, insurance coverage, transport support, and flexible work or school arrangements.

### Health-System Changes Needed

Health-system change underpins all five levers. The starting point is the data gap: Zimbabwe has little population-level CKD data, and a national CKD registry plus screening data from NCD and HIV clinics would show the true burden and guide funding.

1. **National CKD strategy** built into existing NCD and HIV programmes.
2. **Reliable essential medicines:** ACE inhibitors, iron, bicarbonate and other core CKD drugs stocked consistently.
3. **Decentralised expertise:** train district nurses and doctors and link them to nephrologists by telemedicine.
4. **Financing:** insurance or subsidy schemes for dialysis and transplant, so cost does not decide survival.
5. **Upstream prevention:** better hypertension, diabetes and HIV control means less CKD later.

### Take-Home Messages

1. About 1 in 7 adults in pooled African studies has CKD (13.7% in the latest 2026 analysis). Zimbabwe-specific data are limited and urgently needed.
2. Hypertension, diabetes, HIV, infections, obstruction and nephrotoxic drugs drive most disease.
3. Once nephrons are lost, hyperfiltration keeps the damage going, so early action matters most.
4. Quality of life improves when we find CKD early, slow the decline, treat complications, widen access to care and support the whole person.
