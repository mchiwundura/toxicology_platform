# Speaker Background

Dr Margaret Siyawamwaya is a lecturer in Pharmaceutics at the University of Zimbabwe and a registered pharmacist with a strong interest in paediatric pharmacy and medication safety.

## Session Summary

Children are not small adults, and a medicine that is available is of little use if a child cannot or will not take it. This workshop looked at how pharmacists can make the manipulation and administration of paediatric medicines safer, particularly where child-appropriate formulations are limited.

### Why Children Are at High Risk

- Children differ from adults in body composition, organ maturation, and drug clearance, and these differences change across age groups from preterm neonates to adolescents.
- Small dose errors can cause major harm, and weight-based dosing increases the risk.
- Limited paediatric formulations, dependence on caregivers for administration, and communication barriers add to the problem.

### The Access Gap

Access to medicines has five dimensions: physical availability, affordability, geographical accessibility, quality, and **acceptability**. Child-appropriate medicines should be chewable, palatable, dispersible, crushable, and allow dose adjustment. The presentation highlighted these gaps:

- **Off-label use:** 50–90% of paediatric medicines are used off-label (WHO, 2023).
- **Formulation gap:** only about 30% of essential medicines have child-friendly formulations.
- **Knowledge gap:** only 11% of clinical studies are conducted in children.
- **Access gap:** fewer than 50% of children in Sub-Saharan Africa receive needed treatments (UNICEF).
- **R&D gap:** only 4% of pharmaceutical R&D targets paediatric formulations.

### Improvising Through Manipulating Medicines

Because suitable products are often unavailable, tablets and capsules are crushed, dispersed, or mixed into liquids. In paediatrics, the liquid used and the solution made can determine whether treatment succeeds, fails, or causes harm. Other countries have responded by standardising manipulation practices, setting up local specials manufacturers, and supporting smaller companies that specialise in paediatric formulations. For Zimbabwe, solutions should be designed around locally available products, resources, and preferences.

**Local example:** isoniazid liquid medicines from a local health facility appeared physically and chemically stable for 7 days. Yeast and moulds were detected at day 14, as was a positive E. coli test, with humans or water as possible sources.

### Before Manipulating a Formulation, Ask

1. Can the child really not swallow the tablet or capsule?
2. Is a licensed liquid available?
3. Is the medicine modified-release or enteric coated?
4. Is crushing contraindicated?

### Pharmaceutical Considerations

1. **Effect of pH on stability:** some medicines are acid- or alkaline-sensitive (e.g. omeprazole/esomeprazole granules). Damaged coatings or incorrect mixing can reduce effectiveness, and the wrong liquid may inactivate the medicine before absorption.
2. **Effect of salt forms on solubility:** in a case involving a young child with a brain tumour, prochlorperazine mesilate liquid (solubility ≈ 2000 mg/mL) was unavailable and the maleate tablets (≈ 0.015 mg/mL) were crushed instead. The crushed tablets gave an unreliable dose and uncontrolled vomiting. A crushed tablet is not equivalent to a commercial liquid preparation.
3. **Osmolality and GI tolerance:** hyperosmolar liquids, or syrups diluted incorrectly, can cause diarrhoea, abdominal distension, and feeding intolerance. Premature infants are particularly vulnerable, and liquids for neonatal use should have an osmolality of ≤ 450 mOsm/kg.
4. **Dose accuracy:** mixing medicine into full bottles of milk or juice makes intake unpredictable, so medicines should generally be mixed with only a small and/or measured amount of food or liquid. Household teaspoons vary in volume and give inaccurate doses, so use dosing devices where possible and make sure caregivers know how to measure.
5. **Using appropriate liquids:**

| Liquid used | Potential problem |
|---|---|
| Milk/dairy | Reduced absorption of some medicines (e.g. ciprofloxacin, iron) |
| Juice | pH interaction |
| Tea | Reduced iron absorption |
| Thick porridge | Incomplete dose delivery |
| Large feed volumes | Child may not finish the dose |

Extemporaneous preparations also carry risks of degradation, microbial contamination, sedimentation, and reduced potency.

### Conclusions and Call to Action

Safe paediatric medicine administration is not about having perfect systems. It is about pharmacists creating reliable safety checks where children are most vulnerable, through:

- Ward guidelines
- Local SOPs
- Counselling aids for caregivers
- Initiatives for dosing devices
- Administration checklists
