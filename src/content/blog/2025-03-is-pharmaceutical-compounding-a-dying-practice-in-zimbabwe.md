## Introduction

Pharmaceutical compounding — the art and science of preparing personalised medications — has played a vital role in healthcare for centuries. However, in recent years, its prevalence has significantly declined worldwide. This trend is particularly noticeable in Zimbabwe and the broader Southern African region. While compounding remains essential in certain medical scenarios, several economic, regulatory, and practical challenges have led many pharmacists to step away from this practice. This article explores the current state of pharmaceutical compounding, factors contributing to its decline, remuneration models, and strategies to revitalise this crucial aspect of pharmacy practice.

## The decline of pharmaceutical compounding

Historically, nearly half of all medical prescriptions were compounded by pharmacists, addressing specific patient needs that mass-produced medications could not meet. Today, this figure has decreased dramatically due to the rise of commercial pharmaceutical products, which offer convenience and standardisation. In Zimbabwe, the economic instability and limited local pharmaceutical manufacturing capabilities further exacerbate this decline. Additionally, the focus on generic drug production has shifted attention away from personalised medications, reducing the demand for compounding services.

## Challenges faced by pharmacists

Several key challenges have contributed to the waning interest in pharmaceutical compounding:

1. **Resource limitations:** Compounding requires specialised equipment, high-quality raw materials, and rigorous quality control processes. In resource-constrained environments like Zimbabwe, obtaining and maintaining these essentials is often difficult.
2. **Regulatory hurdles:** The absence of comprehensive regulatory frameworks makes it challenging to ensure the standardisation and quality of compounded medications. This lack of oversight can lead to legal risks and concerns about product safety.
3. **Economic constraints:** Given the modest remuneration for pharmacists in Zimbabwe — averaging approximately \$6.62 per hour or between \$12,000 and \$23,000 annually — the time and effort required for compounding may not be financially viable. Many pharmacists may prefer to focus on dispensing commercially available medications that are more profitable and less time-consuming.
4. **Professional training and confidence:** Limited opportunities for specialised training in compounding can reduce pharmacists' confidence and competence in this area, further discouraging its practice.

## Remuneration models for compounding pharmacists

The financial viability of pharmaceutical compounding depends largely on remuneration models that fairly compensate pharmacists for their time, expertise, and resources. In regions where compounding is still prevalent, pharmacists typically charge a fee that reflects the complexity of the preparation, the cost of raw materials, and the time involved. However, in Zimbabwe, the current compensation structure may not adequately reward the additional effort required for compounding, making it less attractive as a professional pursuit.

## Strategies to revitalise pharmaceutical compounding

To reverse the decline of pharmaceutical compounding and harness its benefits for patient care, several measures can be implemented:

1. **Develop clear regulatory guidelines:** Establish comprehensive regulations to standardise compounding practices, ensuring product quality and patient safety while reducing legal risks for pharmacists.
2. **Enhance training and education:** Integrate compounding modules into pharmacy curricula and provide continuing professional development opportunities. Specialised training programmes can boost pharmacists' confidence and competence, encouraging more professionals to engage in compounding.
3. **Introduce economic incentives:** Offer financial incentives such as subsidies for raw materials, tax breaks, or reimbursement schemes for compounded medications. These measures can make compounding more financially viable, attracting more pharmacists to the practice.
4. **Invest in infrastructure:** Allocate resources to improve access to high-quality raw materials and modern equipment, enabling pharmacists to produce safe and effective compounded medications.
5. **Foster professional collaboration:** Establish or strengthen professional associations dedicated to compounding, such as the Association of Compounding Pharmacists of South Africa (ACPSA). These organisations can provide support, advocacy, and networking opportunities for pharmacists interested in compounding.

## Conclusion

While pharmaceutical compounding has experienced a decline in Zimbabwe and Southern Africa, it remains a vital component of personalised healthcare. Addressing the challenges faced by pharmacists through regulatory support, enhanced training, economic incentives, and improved infrastructure can help revitalise this essential practice. By embracing these measures, the region can ensure that patients continue to benefit from tailored medications that meet their unique needs, ultimately enhancing the quality of healthcare available to all.
