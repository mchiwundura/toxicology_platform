## Introduction

Gepotidacin is a novel antibiotic and the first in its class of triazaacenaphthylene antibiotics, currently being developed by GlaxoSmithKline (GSK). With the alarming rise of antimicrobial resistance, particularly among Gram-negative bacteria, traditional antibiotics are becoming less effective in treating infections. Gepotidacin presents a promising solution in the fight against multidrug-resistant strains.

## The need for new antibiotics

The global surge in antimicrobial resistance poses a significant public health threat. A major concern is resistance in Gram-negative bacteria such as *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Enterobacter* spp., and *Escherichia coli* (*E. coli*), with *E. coli* ranked second in bacterial infection-related mortality. Among the most common infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria are urinary tract infections (UTIs) and urogenital gonorrhoea. Treatment options for these infections are increasingly limited due to rising resistance.

Current treatment regimens for resistant infections often involve parenteral antibiotics, which are less convenient, more costly, and may require hospitalisation. This highlights the urgent need for safe and effective oral antibiotic alternatives like gepotidacin.

## What is gepotidacin?

Gepotidacin is an orally administered antibiotic with a unique chemical structure belonging to the triazaacenaphthylene class. It offers a novel mechanism of action, distinguishing it from existing antibiotics.

## Mechanism of action

Gepotidacin inhibits bacterial DNA replication by targeting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV — essential enzymes for bacterial DNA replication and repair. Specifically, it selectively inhibits the B subunit of DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, exerting a bactericidal effect.

Although fluoroquinolones, such as ciprofloxacin, target similar enzymes, in vitro studies have demonstrated that gepotidacin remains active against fluoroquinolone-resistant bacterial strains.

## Clinical trials and results

### UTI trials

Two Phase 3 clinical trials, EAGLE-2 and EAGLE-3, compared the efficacy and safety of gepotidacin with nitrofurantoin in treating uncomplicated urinary tract infections (uUTIs). Patients received either:

- Gepotidacin 1500 mg twice daily for five days, or
- Nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for five days.

Results demonstrated that gepotidacin was non-inferior in one trial and superior in the other, confirming its effectiveness in treating uUTIs with an acceptable safety profile.

### Gonorrhoea trials

A Phase 2 clinical trial enrolled 69 adult participants who were randomised to receive either 1500 mg or 3000 mg of a single oral dose of gepotidacin. Treatment success rates were 97% and 95% for the 1500 mg and 3000 mg groups, respectively. The most common adverse effects reported included diarrhoea, flatulence, nausea, and abdominal pain, occurring in 52% of patients in the 1500 mg group and 64% in the 3000 mg group.

A Phase 3 clinical trial, EAGLE-1, compared gepotidacin to the standard combination therapy of ceftriaxone (500 mg intramuscularly) plus azithromycin (1 g orally) for gonorrhoea treatment. Patients in the gepotidacin group received an initial oral dose of 3000 mg, followed by a second dose of 3000 mg 10–12 hours later. Follow-up visits were conducted on days 4–8 and 14–21. Results showed that gepotidacin was non-inferior to ceftriaxone plus azithromycin, confirming its potential as an alternative treatment for gonorrhoea, especially in cases of antibiotic resistance.

## Safety profile

From clinical trials, the most commonly reported adverse effects were gastrointestinal-related, including diarrhoea, flatulence, nausea, and abdominal pain. No significant safety concerns have been identified to date, but long-term surveillance will be required to fully establish its safety profile.

## Potential impact

The discovery of gepotidacin in a new antibiotic class could pave the way for further research and development of triazaacenaphthylene antibiotics.

Key advantages include:

- **Oral administration:** Offers a convenient alternative to injectable antibiotics, particularly for patients with needle phobia or allergies to cephalosporins.
- **Reduced patient waiting time:** Eliminates the need for reconstitution and administration by healthcare providers, as patients can take the medication at home.
- **Potential for combination therapy:** Gepotidacin may be used alongside other antibiotics to combat multidrug-resistant pathogens.

## Challenges and future directions

Despite its promise, several challenges remain:

- **Regulatory approval:** Gepotidacin is still undergoing review by regulatory authorities, and approval is not guaranteed.
- **Cost and accessibility:** Once approved, initial pricing and patent protection may limit accessibility, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.
- **Antibiotic stewardship:** To prevent resistance, appropriate use of gepotidacin is crucial. Continued antimicrobial stewardship efforts will be necessary to ensure its long-term efficacy.

Additionally, given its strong activity against Gram-negative bacteria, gepotidacin holds potential for treating other infections such as respiratory tract infections, intra-abdominal infections, biliary tract infections, and brain abscesses.

## Conclusion

The discovery of gepotidacin underscores the importance of ongoing research in antibiotic development to combat antimicrobial resistance. Bringing new antibiotics to market requires collaboration between governments, regulatory bodies, and pharmaceutical companies, all while ensuring patient safety.

As healthcare professionals, our role is to prescribe and dispense antibiotics responsibly. Educating patients on antimicrobial resistance is equally critical in safeguarding these vital drugs. By practising prudent antibiotic use, we can help preserve the efficacy of antibiotics — including the promising future of gepotidacin.
